That's where this briefing is meant to help: not by offering predictions or prescriptions, but by grounding decisions in observed reality.

Methodology

This briefing draws on two complementary data sources: aggregated, anonymised industry booking data and a comprehensive Annual Travel Trends Survey (2025). Together, these sources combine observed booking behaviour at scale with reported operator priorities, expectations, and decision-making processes.

Industry Booking Data

Observed booking behaviour drawn from aggregated and anonymised data across major booking platforms, reflecting activity from more than 350,000 multi-day trips, 800,000 bookings, and 1.3 million travellers booked during 2025 for 2026 departures. This data captures how multi-day travel is being sold and booked in practice.

Annual Travel Trends Survey

Conducted between November and December 2025, collecting responses from 427 global multi-day travel businesses. Survey questions focused primarily on trips departing in 2026, with select questions asking operators to reflect on observed booking activity and operational changes during 2025.

Internal platform data reflects observed behaviour across industry networks, but does not represent the entire global travel market. The insights in this briefing are intended to function as directional benchmarks for multi-day travel businesses navigating 2026.

Chapter 1

Travel Costs Rise, Expectations Follow

Higher trip prices are raising traveller expectations, shifting booking behaviour, and increasing the complexity of delivering multi-day travel.

Rising travel costs are shaping the multi-day travel landscape in 2026. As prices climb, travellers are committing differently; booking together more often, expecting more from the experience, and looking for trips that clearly feel worth the investment.

For operators, this translates into more travellers per booking, stronger demand for customization, and greater emphasis on immersive, more meaningful experiences.

Delivering trips that meet these expectations now requires more coordination and precision – increasing the operational complexity behind every departure.

1.6 – What Does This Mean for Travel Businesses?

1 Make Customization Easy for Travellers and Operators

Customization is now a baseline expectation. Allowing travellers to select and pay for add-ons at booking makes it easy for them to shape a trip around their preferences. For operators, using an AI-powered itinerary builder built on reusable components makes it faster to personalize trips and respond to requests without rebuilding itineraries from scratch.

2 Use Payment Plans to Support Higher Prices

With travellers committing to higher-priced trips by paying over time, payment plans are no longer optional. Operators should ensure their booking and payment system supports installments by default, so travellers can commit without completing the full payment upfront, and operators aren't manually tracking deposits, balances, or due dates.

3 Strengthen Your Partner Network as Trips Diversify

More experience-driven and blended itineraries often require partners outside an operator's traditional network. Operators who can easily find, vet, and work with a wider range of local partners (guides, activity providers, community hosts, or specialists) are better positioned to add new experience elements without increasing delivery risk or spending hours searching for and vetting suppliers.

Chapter 2

The Definition of Luxury Is Changing

Custom and luxury travel is expanding as more trips are designed to be immersive, flexible, and intentionally crafted – not just expensive.

Custom and luxury travel is a growing market segment, but not simply because travellers are spending more or moving upmarket. Instead, growth is being shaped by a new definition of luxury – one that no longer only means yacht charters or Michelin-starred dining, but also includes trips shaped by what's important to each group.

For operators, this shift doesn't point to a single model to follow. Some are leaning into high-touch, bespoke planning, while others are balancing personalization with efficiency through selective use of technology.

What unites these approaches is the need for clarity: understanding who their travellers are, what kind of custom experience they value, and how to deliver it sustainably within their business model.

2.4 – What Does This Mean for Travel Businesses?

1 Put Personalization on Repeat

Luxury is increasingly defined by what feels personal to each traveller. This means the operators best positioned to capitalize on the growing demand for custom and luxury trips are those who don't just know their travellers' basic information, but can capture, synthesize, and reuse detailed traveller preferences at scale.

  • •Include intentional questions within every trip inquiry form
  • •Keep track of all traveller information in one place
  • •Create a simple system to find and reuse traveller data

2 Leverage Technology to Scale

Manual personalization is time-intensive and costly; invest in technology that can help sustainably customize trips at scale:

  • •A travel-specific CRM to capture and organize traveller preferences
  • •An AI-powered itinerary builder to save time
  • •Automations for repetitive tasks

3 Offer Custom, Without Overhauling Your Model

For operators considering expanding into this higher-value market segment, start with small steps: test personalization through flexible add-ons, offer custom packages of existing trips, and grow an on-the-ground network of trusted local experts.

Chapter 3

Direct Bookings Dominate, Discovery Diversifies

Trip discovery is spreading across new channels, while bookings concentrate on operators' websites.

This year's data highlights that AI-powered search tools (such as ChatGPT and Google Gemini) and social media platforms will play a significant role in how travellers discover multi-day trips. As trip discovery expands into new environments, travellers are being directed to operator websites to book.

For operators, the implication is practical: be easy to find wherever travellers are searching, and make it simple to book once they arrive.

When websites, content, and social profiles are structured to surface clearly in search engines, AI search, and social platforms – and paired with simple, direct paths to book – operators are better positioned to convert interest into confirmed bookings.

Chapter 4

AI Moves Closer to the Trip

AI is no longer confined to marketing and internal efficiency – it now also supports itinerary planning and early-stage trip delivery.

Last year, AI use in multi-day travel was largely concentrated in marketing, sales preparation, and internal operations.

This year, its role is expanding into workflows that directly shape the trip, including itinerary building, proposals, and early-stage planning. Importantly, these new applications don't replace earlier use cases; they build on them, extending AI's role across the planning journey.

As travellers increasingly expect more personalized trips, this shift matters most in the early stages of planning, where research, revisions, and coordination are most time-intensive. By supporting this work, AI enables operators to deliver greater customization without increasing manual workload.

4.5 – What Does This Mean for Travel Businesses?

1 Start Using AI as Part of Everyday Operations

Operators who are not yet using AI don't need to overhaul their operations, but they should begin integrating it into everyday workflows. This is easier than ever, thanks to tools that integrate AI into existing software and platforms.

2 Grow Bookings with Faster, More Personal Proposals

A travel-specific CRM helps operators sell more trips by keeping traveller history, preferences, and past conversations in one place, making it easier to personalize outreach and proposals without starting from scratch each time.

3 Support Customization Without Sacrificing Time or Margins

Using AI-supported tools to assemble itineraries, iterate quickly on changes, and automatically reflect updates in one shared version reduces manual work across revisions. When itinerary changes are updated in real time for both teams and travellers, operators spend less time on follow-ups and admin, and more time selling and delivering trips.
